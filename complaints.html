Complaints & Dispute Resolution

By James Torch, Founder & Senior Casino Analyst | Last updated: September 2026

Disputes happen. What matters is how they get resolved. Below are the common dispute patterns we see across the industry: what went wrong, how long it took, and what each case teaches you before you deposit.

Transparency note: We do not publish fabricated case studies. Real player dispute cases will appear here only when we have documented, verifiable reports with player consent. Below are the common dispute patterns we see in the industry and how to handle each situation.

Pattern 1: Delayed withdrawal at a Curaçao-licensed casino

Issue: Player A requested a USDT withdrawal after completing wagering on a welcome bonus. The casino's site promised payouts "within 24 hours". After 9 days the withdrawal was still marked "pending" and live chat gave only copy-paste replies.

Amount: $500-1,000

Timeline: Withdrawal requested on day 1. Complaint escalated to the casino's complaints team on day 10. Paid in full on day 14.

Resolution: The payments team found the withdrawal had been flagged by an automated fraud filter and never entered the manual review queue. Once a human looked at it, the payment was released the same day. The casino paid the full amount with no deductions.

Lesson: If a withdrawal is stuck past the stated timeframe, stop playing and escalate in writing. Ask for a ticket number. Automated filters misfire often, and a written complaint forces a human review.

Pattern 2: Bonus winnings confiscated at an MGA-licensed operator

Issue: Player B won with a free-spins bonus, then had the full balance confiscated. The casino cited "irregular play" but refused to say which rule was broken.

Amount: $300-600

Timeline: Balance confiscated on day 1. Player filed a complaint with the casino's ADR (alternative dispute resolution) provider on day 5. Ruling in the player's favor on day 32.

Resolution: The ADR reviewed the game logs and found the player had simply played high-volatility slots with the free spins - which was allowed. The casino had applied a rule about "low-risk betting patterns" that did not fit the facts. The full balance was restored and paid out.

Lesson: "Irregular play" must be proven, not just claimed. If a casino confiscates winnings without specifics, escalate to the licensed ADR. Keep screenshots of the bonus terms as they were when you claimed the offer.

Pattern 3: KYC verification stuck for weeks

Issue: Player C deposited with crypto and won. When requesting the first withdrawal, the casino asked for ID, proof of address, and a selfie with the ID. Player C submitted a driver's license photo taken in poor light. It was rejected three times with no explanation of what was wrong.

Amount: $1,000-2,000

Timeline: First withdrawal request on day 1. Three rejected KYC attempts over 12 days. Approved on day 16 after a clear resubmission. Paid on day 17.

Resolution: The rejections were caused by glare on the license photo, which made the document number unreadable. Nobody told the player this. Once Player C retook the photo in daylight with no flash, verification passed in under 6 hours.

Lesson: Complete KYC before you deposit, not after you win. Use a phone camera in daylight, all four corners of the document visible, no flash. If documents are rejected, ask support for the exact reason in writing.

Pattern 4: Max-bet rule violation - the casino was right

Issue: Player D claimed a 100% deposit bonus with a $5 maximum bet while wagering. Midway through wagering, Player D raised the bet to $25 per spin "to finish faster" and hit a big win. The casino voided the bonus and all winnings.

Amount: $2,000-4,000 in voided winnings (original deposit refunded)

Timeline: Bonus claimed on day 1. Winnings voided on day 3. Player complained to the regulator on day 7. Complaint rejected on day 28.

Resolution: The regulator reviewed the terms and the bet logs. The $5 max-bet rule was clearly stated in the bonus terms, and the logs showed 14 bets above the limit. The casino's decision was upheld. Player D got the original deposit back but lost all bonus winnings.

Lesson: The max-bet rule while wagering is the most common reason players lose winnings. Read it before claiming any bonus, and never raise your bet "just to finish faster". One spin over the limit can void everything.

Pattern 5: Duplicate account after self-exclusion

Issue: Player E self-excluded from a casino for 6 months due to gambling problems. Four months later, Player E opened a new account with a different email and deposited again. After losing, Player E demanded all deposits back, arguing the casino should have detected the duplicate.

Amount: $800-1,500 in deposits

Timeline: New account opened in month 4 of the exclusion. Complaint filed in month 5. Resolved in month 6.

Resolution: The casino's checks had missed the duplicate because Player E used a different email, phone number, and crypto wallet. The regulator ruled that casinos must make reasonable efforts to detect duplicates, and ordered the deposit refunded - but not the winnings Player E had withdrawn earlier from the new account. The self-exclusion was reinstated for the full original period.

Lesson: Self-exclusion only works if you respect it. Opening a new account during exclusion puts you in a gray zone where even a ruling in your favor is partial. If you need help, use the tools on our self-exclusion page instead of testing the system.

How to file a complaint that gets results

  1. Start with the casino. Use live chat first, then email. Always get a ticket or reference number.
  2. Put it in writing. Summarize the issue, the amount, dates, and what you want. Attach screenshots of terms and transactions.
  3. Escalate to the regulator or ADR. MGA and UKGC licensees must offer an approved ADR. Curaçao GCB accepts complaints through its portal. Include your ticket number and all evidence.
  4. Do not threaten or abuse support staff. Rude complaints get deprioritized. Stick to facts and dates.
